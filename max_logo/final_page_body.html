01 — Genre selection

Why horror

I chose a horror/thriller hybrid for my final film, and I decided to make it on my own.

Fear is a core emotion

Horror creates a powerful emotion: fear. Our society has become so safe that we rarely feel real fear, and that makes it special.

I have done it before

My preliminary was also a horror film, so I already have some experience with the genre.

The setting is already there

Where I live is mountainous and isolated, which suits a horror atmosphere. All I need is cameras and a drone, and the only actor is me.

Working alone

I wanted full control over my film and no creative differences, and I want to improve every one of my filmmaking skills.

Also considered

Dystopian / sci-fi: the props and sets would be too difficult to organise. Mystery: a detective story would be cool, but horror is more fun.

02 — Studio name & logo

M&M Productions

Short and impactful, which fits the horror/thriller genre. The logo is classy: white letters on black for contrast, with one subtle horror detail, the blood drop.

Mission Make Horror Great Again

Today's horror is oversaturated with formulaic, soulless blockbusters that rely on cheap jump scares and corporate safe bets rather than genuine terror. M&M exists to break the modern horror taboo and restore the genre's raw, unsettling power.

Our core idea: great cinema before raw profit. We don't make movies to please box-office algorithms. We make horror for the love of the craft, creating experiences that haunt audiences long after the screen goes black.

03 — Audience & marketing

Who it's for

Phasmophobia is a very popular indie game with over 30 million copies sold, and most of its players are aged 13–25. So the film's main aim is to attract teens and young adults. People over 30 are less likely to watch, but older horror fans may still come for a ghost story.

Girls 15–16

Positive about horror and likely to watch it, but most don't know the game. Ghosts are a decent antagonist, though real-life experiences feel more personal. Mixed about game films: they have to be done right. They find films on TikTok, some on Letterboxd.

Boys 15–16

Many know the game and are very positive about a Phasmophobia film. They love the thrill of fear, but find horror oversaturated and prefer older horror. Game films are seen as "cheap" and hard to do. Mostly TikTok.

Over 30

Mostly don't like horror and are very unlikely to watch it. They find game films uncreative and less credible, and are scared most by psychological, realistic situations. They hear about films through newspapers and A24.

What I learned
  • Break the taboo. Fans of the game want originality and good execution.
  • "Inspired by", not "based on". It is safer for every audience and makes the film more original.
  • Keep it realistic. People are scared by psychological impact more than by jump scares or blood, so the mood and setting matter most.
  • Make the ghost a mystery, like in The Shining. Is there really a ghost, or is it imagination?

Online

TikTok and Instagram, the platforms young people use most, plus Letterboxd.

Around town

Posters and billboards in busy entertainment areas. For Marbella, that means Puerto Banús.

04 — Genre research

Horror, then and now

How horror changed over a century, what is missing from it today, and what makes a horror film memorable.

  1. 1920sGothic literature and German Expressionism: shadows and distorted sets. The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari, Nosferatu.
  2. 1930s–40sHollywood's classic monsters: Dracula, Frankenstein.
  3. 1950sFears of science, nuclear weapons and alien invasion, reflecting the Cold War.
  4. 1960sPsychological horror: Psycho showed ordinary people can be monsters.
  5. 1970s–80sGraphic slashers and masked killers: Halloween, A Nightmare on Elm Street.
  6. 1990sScream reinvented the slasher with humour and self-awareness.
  7. 2000sFound footage and unseen threats: Paranormal Activity.
  8. TodayPsychological tension, social issues and ambiguity: Get Out, Hereditary.

What's missing? Steve Neale's genre theory: repetition and difference

Horror is popular, but popularity has created a problem: more sequels, reboots and familiar ideas. A 2025 Reuters report found horror made up 17% of North American cinema ticket sales, up from 4% a decade earlier, yet audiences are losing interest in mediocre, repetitive films.

06Sound-led horror rather than jump scares

Five ingredients of a recognisable horror film

1

Signature visual

A mask, object, creature or symbol you recognise instantly.

2

Signature sound

A noise, melody, rhythm or silence tied to the threat.

3

Unique fear

A fear people understand but haven't seen explored this way.

4

Its own rules

The audience slowly learns what causes the danger, and what happens if the rules are broken.

5

Central mystery

A question that keeps you watching and talking afterwards.

My plan
  • Work on gap 6 (sound-led horror) and possibly gap 3, using ghost and mirror folklore.
  • A recognisable sound motif is the ingredient I can build into my script most easily.

Horror clichés I'll avoid

The fake-out jump scare

Quiet tension builds to a deafening music sting, and it's just a cat jumping out of a closet.

"Let's split up"

Characters hear a terrifying noise in a dark house and investigate alone without turning on the lights.

Lots of gore

A blood-heavy script that relies on disgust instead of fear.

Pure innocence vs pure evil

A spotless victim hunted by an evil monster, or an evil person punished like in Saw. It is meant to create sympathy, but it makes the story two-dimensional and boring.

Simple supernatural villains

Plain zombies, vampires and ghosts are overused and have no depth. A hybrid or an original monster, like in Stranger Things, works better.

05 — Film openings

Looking at the greats

Three famous horror openings, broken down by mise-en-scène, sound, cinematography and lighting.

1980 · Stanley Kubrick

The Shining

Aerial shots follow a small yellow car through an isolated mountain landscape to the Overlook Hotel. No dialogue, just beautiful but lonely scenery and unsettling music. The hotel is finally revealed, setting up isolation and mystery.

Establishing shot
Extreme long / aerial shot. A vast lake among mountains makes the setting feel enormous and isolated.
Mise-en-scène
Natural setting. Mountains, forest and water suggest the characters will be cut off from civilisation.
Cinematography
Aerial tracking shot. Following the car gives the impression that something is watching it.
Symbolic code
The small yellow car looks insignificant in the landscape: its passengers are vulnerable.
Setting
The isolated hotel. Surrounded by wilderness, so escape or help would be difficult.
Sound
Non-diegetic score. Unsettling orchestral music against beautiful scenery tells us something is wrong before anything happens.
1978 · John Carpenter

Halloween

An animated jack-o'-lantern on black, with the iconic theme. Then Haddonfield, Illinois, and a first-person POV shot approaching a suburban house (from about 2:06). We see through the eyes of an unknown observer, and the first four minutes show only the start of the stalking, never the killer.

Mise-en-scène
The jack-o'-lantern instantly sets up Halloween imagery and a recognisable horror identity.
Sound
Non-diegetic piano theme. Repetitive and unsettling, it builds suspense before the story begins.
Cinematography
POV and tracking shots. We see through the stranger's eyes as the camera circles the house, so we feel like we are the stalker.
Lighting
Low-key. Darkness and shadow hide details, so we can't tell what's hiding.
Narrative enigma
An unidentified observer. We don't know who is watching or why, which keeps us watching.
1996 · Wes Craven

Scream

Casey Becker, a teenager home alone, answers a call from an unknown man. It starts friendly, then he turns threatening, asks her about horror films and hints that he knows where she is.

Mise-en-scène
The telephone. An ordinary household object becomes the source of danger and the start of the horror.
Sound
Diegetic ringing. Every ring builds anticipation and makes us uncomfortable.
Cinematography
Close-up. We watch Casey's face change as the call turns threatening.
Lighting
Warm domestic light. The normal-looking home contrasts with the threat: danger can enter an ordinary house.
Symbolic code
The knives. She plays with them casually; knives connote murder and foreshadow her death.
Framing
Rule of thirds. Casey and the knives sit on the thirds, linking her to them.

07 — Script

Spectrophobia

A tired ghost hunter who doesn't believe in ghosts takes one more late-night call.

Title

The M&M Productions ident (5–8 s), then "presents… Spectrophobia" in a white, scary font as the music starts. Fade to black.

Scene 1Inside the car · night

I'm asleep in my car, snoring, in a grey work uniform. The car is messy: coffee in the cup holder, radios and UV lights everywhere, every mirror open plus one or two loose mirrors. Mid shot, rule of thirds. Seven seconds of snoring (maybe crickets) while the credits roll in cyan.

A phone rings, at 4 AM. I gasp, breathe out and pick up an old phone, wired or a Nokia. The camera switches to my other side.

"M&M Express Ghost Hunting, how can I help you?" (tired; the caller is just a mumble, added later)

"OK ma'am, sounds like a simple poltergeist. Could you give me the address? I'll come as soon as possible."

POV shot of me writing 13 Willow Street. "Uh huh, 13 Willow Street." Side view: "OK, I won't be longer than 30 minutes." I hang up, sigh and start the engine. Fade to black.

Scene 2Mountain road · dusk

Fade in on a high, wide drone shot (10–15 s) of my car on a narrow mountain road as it gets dark, showing isolation. Cut to inside, camera on the back seat: headlights on, light-hearted music, and a voice-over:

"Everyone thinks they have ghosts. Poltergeists, demons, yokai. Some people just can't accept that things fall sometimes, and houses creak at night. My job is to come over, throw some holy water around, wave a crucifix, leave, and make easy money."

Scene 3The gate

The car passes an abandoned construction site. Five seconds later I pull up to the gate of a big house with every light off. I get out: "Hello? Anybody home?" After about three seconds I spot the doorbell camera and ring it.

Cut to the doorbell camera's footage: "Hello, I'm the ghost hunter. This is 13 Willow Street, right?" The doorbell beeps and the gate opens, but nobody answers. "Finally." I drive in.

Scene 4The driveway

From inside the driveway, the car arrives and parks. From behind me through the windscreen, the full-beam headlights light up the front door, and bloody handprints on the door and windows. I unbuckle, grab the crucifix from the cup holder and get out. Cut to black.
